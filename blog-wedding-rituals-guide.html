Traditions

A simple guide to the wedding rituals, for the whole family

A wedding does not belong only to the couple, it belongs to a whole line of people.

N
Namratha TejkiranCo-founder · Planning & guest experience · May 2026 · 7 min read

If you have ever sat at a South Indian wedding and quietly wondered what the purohit is chanting, why the groom suddenly picks up an umbrella, or when exactly the real moment happens, you are in good company. Most of us grew up watching these rituals without ever being told what they mean. That is a shame, because the meaning is the best part.

We are Versato, an event team based in Bengaluru, and we have stood beside a lot of families on their most important morning. This is our plain, affectionate walk through South Indian wedding rituals explained in the order they usually happen. We have written it for the cousin who is nervous about doing the wrong thing, the friend marrying into the culture, and the grandparent who wants the youngsters to actually understand.

No jargon you cannot follow. No pretending every family does it identically, because they do not. Just what each ceremony is, why it matters, and how to move through the day without your shoulders around your ears.

A wedding does not belong only to the couple, it belongs to a whole line of people.

Before the day: naandi and the quiet blessings

Long before the garlands come out, most families begin with naandi, sometimes called naandi shraddha. This is the moment the family formally invites the blessings of their elders and ancestors before an auspicious event. It is done quietly, usually at home, with the purohit guiding the parents through offerings and prayers. Think of it as asking permission and goodwill from everyone who came before.

It can feel solemn, and it is meant to. Naandi sets the tone: this wedding does not belong only to the couple, it belongs to a whole line of people. If you are marrying into the family, this is a lovely ceremony to simply watch and absorb. You do not need to perform anything.

These blessing rituals sit inside a much bigger family tradition of samskaras, the milestones a Hindu life is marked by. The naamakarana names a newborn, the upanayana marks a young person stepping into study and responsibility, and the wedding follows years later. Seeing the wedding as one link in that chain helps a lot of nervous relatives relax. You are not being tested. You are being welcomed into a story already in progress. If the ritual detail feels overwhelming, our purohit and rituals team exists precisely to explain each step to families as it happens.

Haldi and mehendi: colour, laughter and getting ready

Now the mood lifts. The haldi and mehendi rituals are the warm, noisy, photograph-heavy part of the celebration, and they carry real meaning underneath the fun.

The haldi

Turmeric paste is applied to the bride and groom, often at their own homes, by married women of the family. Turmeric is prized for its glow and its old association with protection and purity, so the ceremony is part blessing, part skincare, part excuse to tease the couple. Expect yellow everywhere. Wear clothes you have made peace with staining. We have written a fuller haldi guide if you want to plan yours properly, including who applies it and in what order.

The mehendi

Mehendi, or henna, is applied to the bride's hands and feet in fine detail, and increasingly to the hands of aunts, sisters and friends too. It is social by design. People sit for hours, sing, eat, and gossip while the paste dries. There is a gentle old belief that the darker the stain, the deeper the affection in the marriage. Nobody enforces it, but everyone quietly checks.

A tip we give every family: schedule mehendi with enough drying time. Rushed henna is faint henna, and a stressed bride is not the goal.

The muhurtham: why the timing rules everything

You will hear the word muhurtham a great deal, so let us settle the wedding muhurtham meaning once and clearly. A muhurtham is an auspicious window of time, calculated by the purohit from the couple's birth charts and the almanac. The most important rituals of the day are timed to fall inside it.

This is why South Indian weddings can start at hours that surprise outsiders. A five in the morning or a mid-morning slot is not the planner being awkward. It is the sky being consulted. In Kannada weddings especially, the whole schedule is built backwards from that sacred window, so everything else has to bend around it.

For family members, the practical takeaway is simple: when someone says the muhurtham is at a certain minute, that minute is not negotiable. Be seated, be quiet, and be present. The photographs of everyone scrambling in late are funny only years later.

The quick version

  • The rituals follow a logic: naandi asks for blessings, haldi and mehendi prepare and celebrate, and the muhurtham rituals bind the marriage.
  • The muhurtham is a fixed auspicious window. Build your whole schedule around it and be seated on time.
  • Mangalya dharana and saptapadi are the true heart of the ceremony, so protect that block of the morning above everything else.
  • Nobody marrying into the culture is expected to perform flawlessly. Watching respectfully is enough, and one relative explaining each step makes all the difference.
  • Calm planning, fed elders, and a clear running order turn a hectic day into a warm one.

Kashi yatra and mangalya dharana: the heart of it

Here we reach the ceremonies most people picture when they think of a South Indian wedding. Two of them tend to steal the morning.

Kashi yatra

This one delights every first-timer. The groom pretends to give up on married life entirely and sets off on a mock pilgrimage to Kashi, umbrella and walking stick in hand, declaring he will pursue a life of study and renunciation instead. The bride's father, or her brother, gently intercepts him and persuades him to return and marry, praising the joys and duties of family life. It is playful, it is old, and it carries a real point: marriage is a considered choice, not a drift. Watch for the umbrella. It always gets a laugh.

Mangalya dharana

Then comes the moment many consider the true centre of the wedding. In mangalya dharana, the groom ties the mangalsutra, the sacred thread or chain, around the bride's neck while the purohit chants and the family showers the couple with rice and blessings. In Kannada tradition this is often surrounded by dhaare, the ritual pouring of sacred water over the joined hands of the couple as the bride is formally given in marriage. When the knots are tied, a cheer usually goes up. That cheer is your cue: the marriage has, in the eyes of the gathering, just happened.

Saptapadi: the seven steps that seal it

If mangalya dharana is the emotional peak, saptapadi is the binding vow. The couple takes seven steps together, usually around the sacred fire, and with each step they make a promise: for nourishment, strength, prosperity, happiness, family, the seasons of life together, and lifelong friendship.

In many traditions it is only after the seventh step that the marriage is considered complete and unbreakable. The fire is the witness. There is something moving about watching two people walk a distance you could cross in three seconds and understand that it is meant to take a lifetime.

After saptapadi come the closing rituals and the blessings of elders, where the couple touches the feet of their parents and grandparents to receive good wishes. Then, mercifully for everyone, food. If you want to understand how all of these pieces fit into one flowing morning, our overview of the full wedding lays out the sequence in more depth.

Planning it calmly: a gentle timeline

Understanding the rituals is half the battle. The other half is arranging them so nobody is frantic. Here is the wedding rituals timeline we tend to work from, loosely, because every family adjusts it.

The weeks before

Fix the muhurtham with your purohit first, then build everything else around it. Naandi, the haldi, and mehendi are usually slotted into the day or two before the wedding. Confirm who is performing which role early, because aunts and uncles like to know their part in advance, and asking on the morning invites chaos.

The wedding morning

Group the rituals sensibly. Kashi yatra, dhaare, mangalya dharana and saptapadi cluster around the muhurtham, so protect that block fiercely. Keep a calm person, family or planner, whispering the running order to whoever is next. The couple should not be managing logistics on their own wedding day.

A few kind rules

  • Feed your elders and your priest on time. Rituals run smoother on a full stomach.
  • Tell guests roughly when the muhurtham falls so the important moments are witnessed, not missed.
  • Assign one relative to explain steps to anyone marrying in. It is a generous job and it means the world.

Do this, and the day stops being a stressful performance and becomes what it is meant to be: your family, present and unhurried, watching two people begin.

What is the most important moment in a South Indian wedding?

+

Most families point to two moments: mangalya dharana, when the groom ties the sacred thread around the bride's neck, and saptapadi, the seven steps that complete the vows. In many traditions the marriage is considered final only after the seventh step. Both fall within the muhurtham, so they are the moments to watch closely.

Why do South Indian weddings start so early in the morning?

+

The main rituals are timed to fall inside the muhurtham, an auspicious window the purohit calculates from the couple's birth charts and the almanac. That window often lands in the early morning. The unusual start time is about honouring the auspicious moment, not about inconveniencing guests.

I am marrying into the family and feel lost. What should I do?

+

Relax and watch. You are not expected to know or perform every ritual, and no one is judging you. Ask one warm relative to sit near you and quietly explain each step, and simply be present and respectful. That is genuinely all that is wanted.

What is kashi yatra and why does the groom pretend to leave?

+

Kashi yatra is a playful ritual where the groom acts out renouncing married life to become a scholar and pilgrim, before the bride's father or brother persuades him to return and marry. It dramatises the idea that marriage is a deliberate choice over a solitary life. It is one of the most light-hearted and photographed moments of the day.
