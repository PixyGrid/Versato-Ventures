Haldi, and how to keep it beautiful and mess-free

A beautiful haldi and an easy-to-clean haldi are the same thing done well.

N
Namratha TejkiranCo-founder · Planning & guest experience · Mar 2026 · 7 min read

A haldi is meant to be a little chaotic. Turmeric on cheeks, laughter, someone smearing paste where it absolutely does not belong. That mess is the point, and we would never take it away from you. What we can do is make sure the mess stays where you want it, so the clean-up is quick and the photos are lovely.

We are Namratha and Sourabh, and at Versato we plan and style haldis across Bengaluru with our own decor, florals and photography team. Over the years we have learned that a beautiful haldi and an easy-to-clean haldi are the same thing done well.

Here is exactly how we set one up, from flooring to outfits to catching the best light.

A beautiful haldi and an easy-to-clean haldi are the same thing done well.

Start with the floor, not the flowers

Everyone plans the backdrop first. We plan the floor first, because turmeric water travels and stains anything porous. Get the base right and half your worry disappears.

For an outdoor haldi on grass or a raw terrace, we lay a washable canvas or a heavy cotton dhurrie under the seating zone, then a layer of durrie or dari mats on top for colour. Indoors, avoid light marble and untreated wood without protection. A large, good-looking tarp dressed with cotton runners and cushions reads as intentional, not practical.

Keep a clear two-foot border of protected floor around wherever the smearing happens. That is the splash zone, and it will earn its name.

Seating that flatters people and photos

Low seating is the heart of a haldi. It looks relaxed, it keeps everyone close, and it photographs beautifully from above.

We like a raised platform or a cluster of firm floor cushions and bolsters for the couple, set slightly forward so guests can reach in to apply the haldi without a scramble. Cover cushions in colours you are happy to see turmeric on: mustard, deep coral, terracotta, warm pink. Pale ivory looks gorgeous for about four minutes.

Give the couple an exit

One thing families forget: the couple needs a clean path to stand up and walk to a rinse point without trailing paste across the whole setup. We always leave a discreet gap in the seating and a runner leading out. Small detail, big difference to your decor and design surviving the afternoon.

Decor beyond marigold

Marigold is the default, and defaults get boring. It is a fine option, but it is only one option, and a haldi gives you room to play with colour that photographs far richer.

We build our haldi backdrops with fresh florals in unexpected pairings: butter-yellow roses with white tuberose, orange lilies against soft green foliage, jasmine strung through a woven cane frame. Our floral team keeps the palette warm without leaning on a single bloom.

Texture does the heavy lifting

Layer in fabric and craft, not just flowers. Think mustard and rust drapes gathered at the corners, handpainted terracotta pots, cane fans, mirror-work cushions, brass urlis floated with petals and diyas. Drapes are also your friend for hiding a protective tarp edge or a less lovely wall. Colour, texture and light will carry the whole look, and none of it minds a splash of turmeric.

The quick version

  • Protect the floor first with washable tarps and cotton runners; the splash zone is real, so plan a two-foot border around it.
  • Seat the couple on low cushions in turmeric-friendly colours and leave a clean exit path to a rinse point.
  • Go beyond marigold: fresh florals, warm drapes, texture and colour photograph richer and hide splashes just as well.
  • Dress in yellows, oranges and forgiving cottons, oil skin and hairline beforehand, and keep a change of clothes ready.
  • Shoot in soft mid-morning or late-afternoon light, and set your wash point and spare covers up before the ceremony starts.

Outfits and stain-friendly styling

Turmeric is a dye. Treat every outfit near the splash zone as something that might carry a golden memory forever, and dress accordingly.

  • Lean into yellow. Yellows, oranges, mustards and greens hide turmeric best. Deep colours forgive more than pastels.
  • Choose forgiving fabrics. Cotton and cotton blends wash better than delicate silk, chiffon or anything with heavy zari. Save the heirloom silk for another function.
  • Have a plan for after. Keep a change of clothes and a couple of old towels ready. Rinse stains with cool water quickly, never hot, and skip the frantic rubbing.

For guests, one line on the invite works wonders: wear something you would not cry over. They will thank you.

Skin and hair, briefly

A little coconut or almond oil on arms and hairline before the paste goes on makes turmeric rinse off far more easily. Keep a basket of it near the seating. It doubles as a sweet, useful favour.

Timing, light and photography

A haldi lives on natural light, so the clock matters as much as the colour. Turmeric glows in soft daylight and looks muddy under harsh midday sun or yellow indoor bulbs.

We schedule outdoor haldis for mid-morning, roughly between nine and eleven, or late afternoon before the light drops. Both give you that gold, flattering glow with no squinting. If your only slot is noon, we set up under a sheer canopy or dappled shade so faces stay soft.

For the photography, tell your team the exact moment the smearing starts. That first handful of haldi, the laughing, the messy hug, those are the frames you will keep. We also love a few overhead shots of the seating and florals before anyone sits, because the setup is never that pristine again. If you want the wider picture of how this fits your whole celebration, our full ritual guide maps out the sequence.

The little touches guests remember

Once the setup is sorted, the haldi becomes a party, and a haldi is one of the easiest functions to make genuinely warm rather than merely pretty. The small things do most of the work here.

Keep the food light and finger-friendly, because nobody wants a full sit-down meal with turmeric on their hands. Think tender coconut, cut fruit, filter coffee, a chaat counter, and a tray of the family's favourite sweets. A dhol or a simple playlist of upbeat, singable songs lifts the energy far more than an expensive sound rig, and it gives the smearing a rhythm to happen to.

We also love a few thoughtful favours that fit the day: a little jar of the oil we mentioned, a hand fan for the heat, a packet of seeds or a small potted plant. If children are coming, give them their own corner with a bit of colour and something to do, and the whole afternoon runs sweeter. None of this is expensive. It is just attention, and attention is what people actually remember about a haldi long after the yellow has washed out.

Clean-up planned before it starts

The families who enjoy their haldi most are the ones who decided how to end it before it began. Clean-up is a setup decision, not an afterthought.

We station a simple wash point nearby: a couple of buckets, a jug, mild soap, and a stack of dark towels. We keep spare covers for the cushions and a bin bag tucked behind the drapes. Because the floor was protected from the start, the actual reset is rolling up runners and shaking out a tarp, not scrubbing a terrace on your knees at sunset. That is the whole trick. Contain the mess, and you get to be fully in the moment instead of watching the marble.

How do I stop turmeric from staining everything at a haldi?

+

Protect surfaces before guests arrive with washable tarps, cotton dhurries and dark cushion covers, and keep a clear border around the smearing area. Ask everyone to wear yellows, oranges or forgiving cottons rather than pale silks. A little oil on skin and hairline helps the paste rinse off easily afterwards.

What is the best time of day for a haldi?

+

Mid-morning, around nine to eleven, or late afternoon before the light drops. Both give you soft, golden natural light that makes turmeric glow and keeps faces flattering. If you can only do midday, set up under a sheer canopy or shade so the light stays gentle.

Do we have to use marigold for haldi decor?

+

Not at all. Marigold is one option among many, and a haldi gives you room to play with colour. We build backdrops with fresh florals like butter-yellow roses and tuberose, warm drapes, terracotta and cane, which photograph richer and cope with turmeric just as happily.

What should the couple wear for a haldi?

+

Choose yellows, mustards, oranges or greens in cotton or cotton blends, which hide turmeric best and wash out more easily than delicate silk or heavy zari. Save heirloom pieces for another function. Keep a change of clothes and dark towels ready so you can rinse and reset quickly.
